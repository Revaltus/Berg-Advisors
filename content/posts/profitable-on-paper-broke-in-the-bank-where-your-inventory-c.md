Most multi-channel sellers who call us say some version of the same thing: the P&L looks great, and the bank balance says otherwise. Both numbers are correct. They are just answering different questions, and the gap between them is almost always sitting in a warehouse, on a boat, or in a marketplace reserve.

This post walks through where that cash goes, in plain English, and how to plan reorders so growth stops feeling like a squeeze.

## Why a purchase order is not an expense

When you pay a supplier $40,000 for a production run, that money leaves your bank account immediately. Your profit and loss statement, though, does not record it as a cost. Under accrual accounting, inventory is an asset. It sits on the balance sheet at what you paid for it until a unit actually sells.

Only then does the cost move into cost of goods sold (COGS) and hit your P&L, matched against the revenue it produced. [IRS Publication 538](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p538) describes the inventory rules behind this treatment for businesses that buy and sell merchandise.

That matching is the right way to measure profit. It also explains the confusion. Inventory purchases vs cost of goods sold is the whole story behind "why am I profitable but out of cash." You spent the money months ago. The profit shows up only as the units leave.

## A cash-conversion example with real numbers

Take a seller of home organization products on Amazon and Shopify. She orders 5,000 units at $8 each, a $40,000 purchase, and sells them at $24.

Here is how the cash actually moves:

| Timeline | What happens | Cash effect |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | 50% deposit to supplier | -$20,000 |
| Day 35 | Production finishes, balance due | -$20,000 |
| Day 35 to 75 | Ocean freight and customs | -$3,500 |
| Day 75 to 90 | Receiving and prep at the warehouse or FBA | -$1,000 |
| Day 90 to 180 | Units sell through | Revenue accrues |
| Marketplace payouts | Roughly 7 to 14 days after sale, net of fees and reserves | Cash returns slowly |

She has spent $44,500 before selling a single unit. At $24 per unit, the full run brings in $120,000 in gross sales. After marketplace fees, ads, and shipping, maybe $45,000 to $55,000 is left over to cover that cost and everything else.

On the P&L, the first 1,000 units sold look wonderful. Revenue is $24,000 and COGS is only about $9,000. But the other 4,000 units, roughly $35,000 of cash, are still sitting on shelves. She is profitable and cannot make payroll for her part-time team.

This stretch from paying the supplier to receiving the payout is the cash conversion cycle for ecommerce. For many sellers it runs 120 to 200 days. Every day it gets longer, you need more cash to support the same sales.

## Why growth makes the problem worse

Here is the contrarian part. The faster you grow, the more cash you tie up.

If sales double next quarter, you need to place a bigger order today, before the extra revenue exists. You are funding growth from the front. A seller doing $100,000 a month with a 150-day cycle needs far more working capital than one doing $50,000 a month with the same cycle.

Owners often respond by reordering whatever sold well last month. That is guesswork, and it is where cash disappears. Three patterns come up again and again:

- **Overbuying the winner.** A SKU spikes during a promotion, so you order six months of stock. The spike fades, and the cash is stuck.
- **Underbuying and stocking out.** You lose rank on the marketplace, then pay for ads to win it back.
- **Rush freight.** You fly units in at three times the ocean cost to avoid a stockout you could have predicted.

None of these show up as a problem on a cash-basis P&L. Many sellers start there, and it can look fine for a while. Once you carry real inventory, it hides the very thing you most need to see.

## What accrual books show you that a bank balance cannot

As a firm, we have spent more than 30 years doing this work, and we are one of the few full-service U.S. firms specializing in e-commerce accrual accounting, inventory, and multi-state sales tax nexus. Here is what clean monthly accrual books give you:

1. **Inventory on the balance sheet, by SKU or category.** You see how much cash is parked in stock and how fast it turns.
2. **Accurate gross margin.** Landed cost (product, freight, duties, prep) flows into COGS, so margin is real, not flattering.
3. **Payables and deposits tracked.** Money owed to suppliers and money already paid out are both visible.
4. **Marketplace receivables and reserves.** Amazon, Shopify, Walmart, and eBay each pay out differently. Reconciling them shows what is owed to you and when it lands.
5. **A monthly close.** Numbers arrive while you can still act on them, not in March of next year.

## Planning reorders around cash instead of guesswork

Before any large purchase order, we work through a short list of questions with owners:

- How many days of stock do we have on hand for this SKU, including units in transit?
- What is the all-in landed cost per unit, and the contribution margin after fees and ads?
- When does the deposit leave the bank, and when does the balance come due?
- What will the bank balance look like at the low point, before sell-through catches up?
- Do we have a line of credit or a credit card float to cover that dip, and what does it cost?

Often the answer is to split one big order into two smaller ones, negotiate a 30/70 deposit instead of 50/50, or ship part by air and part by sea. Sometimes it is to wait. A 13-week cash forecast built on your real sell-through rates makes these trade-offs visible in dollars.

The [SBA's guidance on managing your finances](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/manage-your-finances) makes a similar point: separate profit from cash and forecast both. We simply do it with your SKUs and your suppliers.

## Common Questions

**Why am I profitable but out of cash?**

Because inventory you have paid for but not yet sold is an asset, not an expense. Your cash left when you paid the supplier, but your profit only recognizes the cost as units sell. Add shipping time and marketplace payout delays, and growth can drain cash even in a profitable year.

**What is the cash conversion cycle for ecommerce?**

It is the number of days between paying your supplier and receiving the marketplace payout from the sale. For many product sellers it runs 120 to 200 days. Shortening production deposits, freight time, or sell-through speed frees up working capital.

**When does inventory become cost of goods sold?**

Under accrual accounting, inventory becomes COGS in the month the unit sells, not the month you buy it. Landed costs like freight and duties are included, so your gross margin reflects what each unit truly cost.

**Should I switch from cash to accrual accounting?**

If you carry meaningful inventory, usually yes, at least for management reporting. Banks and investors also tend to require accrual financials. Talk with your CPA about tax method rules before changing how you file.
